Pathways in motion.

Behind every cohort is a learner, a school, a partner, or an employer trying to build something better. In the spring of 2025, twenty-one Hillside middle schoolers spent ten weeks as engineers, designing, building, testing, and defending a bridge across the Hudson River. This page makes those outcomes visible: the human ones and the measurable ones, together.

Aedifica Explore · Hillside Innovation Academy · Building Bridges

From "fixing things" to defending a design.

A Hillside learner began the program describing engineering the way most middle schoolers do, as "fixing things." Over ten weeks, that picture sharpened. Working on one of four teams, each member with an assigned leadership role, they took on a Hudson River bridge challenge: design a prototype, build it, and make the case for why it was the strongest, to a panel of professional civil engineering judges.

Along the way they learned the real vocabulary of structures: tension, compression, buckling, the strongest shapes. They learned that a prototype that fails a test isn't a failure, it's information. And they learned to stand up, explain a decision, and answer a hard question from an engineer who builds things for a living.

"When we mess up, this only sets you up for a new door to open."A Hillside learner, post-program reflection

Three observable outcomes

  1. Completed and defended a team bridge prototype before a panel of professional civil engineering judges.

  2. Articulated a deeper view of engineering: pre/post surveys show the cohort moving from "designing a structure" to "designing, creating, testing, and improving solutions."

  3. Advanced STEM trajectory: 6 of the 11 eighth-grade participants (55%) earned acceptance into the selective Union County Vocational-Technical Schools for 2025-26.

Outcome 3 is a cohort-level result, not attributed to a single learner.

School Story · District implementation

A 10-week elective inside the school day

Building Bridges runs as a 10-week NGSS-aligned science elective inside the school day. Hillside Innovation Academy, a STEM-focused magnet middle school, embedded the program for grades 7-8, reaching about 22% of its enrollment in 2025. HIA's own STEM instructors carried the learning through the week between sessions.

Instructor Story · Curriculum in action

Built, tested, revised: not just watched

Students understood tension, compression, and cost most deeply when they built, tested, and revised, not when they watched. Led by a Stevens assistant professor of civil engineering with a construction-engineering co-instructor, the curriculum traded lectures for two real design challenges: a sticker-design pitch and the Hudson River bridge competition. Mixed-grade teams and clear roles turned a rigorous course into a community.

Partner Story · Hillside × Stevens

Two institutions, one support system

Building Bridges is a Hillside Innovation Academy × Stevens Institute of Technology collaboration, funded by the Engineering Information Foundation, now in its second program year. The 2025 program's full investment ($10,340.29) was independently reviewed and certified through Stevens' Office of Sponsored Accounting and Cost Analysis.

Families filled the room at Stevens

Pathway Story · Toward advanced STEM

On the on-ramp to selective STEM high schools

For eighth graders at a high-school decision point, Building Bridges sits right on the on-ramp to selective STEM high schools. 6 of 11 eighth-grade participants (55%) were accepted into Union County Vocational-Technical Schools for 2025-26, among the most competitive public high schools in the state.

Five shifts, in students' own words.

Drawn from student reflections and the Engineering Information Foundation report, the changes the program team and the data both named.

Engineering became real

Students moved from defining engineering as 'fixing things' to 'designing, creating, testing, and improving solutions.' A visit to Stevens' Innovation Expo showed them the range and genuine rewards of the work.

Engineering is 'building a prototype' and 'using serious critical thinking skills.' Student reflection

Confidence grew through presentation

Preparing for the competition and answering judges' questions built real public-speaking and technical-communication skill. Instructors described it as an unexpected, standout development of the program.

Students "both reported and demonstrated increased confidence in public speaking." EIF report

Failure became part of the design process

Testing prototypes taught students that a failed test is data, not defeat. They came to embrace the messy, iterative nature of real engineering.

"When we mess up, this only sets you up for a new door to open." Student reflection

Teamwork and leadership were practiced

Mixed-grade teams with assigned roles let students discover that leadership can mean listening, helping a teammate, organizing the work, or explaining an idea clearly.

Teamwork, time management, and leadership were "frequently cited" outcomes. EIF report

Pathways became visible

By working alongside undergraduates, graduate researchers, and faculty, students saw engineering as a reachable career with many roles. For the eighth-grade cohort, the next step became concrete: 55% were accepted into selective STEM high schools.

"It is as important to learn as to succeed." Student reflection

An early experience, not an endpoint.

Building Bridges helps a middle schooler see engineering as reachable, and gives them a first taste of a college campus, a real design challenge, and an engineer's questions. For the 2025 eighth-grade cohort, the next step is already visible: more than half earned acceptance into selective STEM high schools.
